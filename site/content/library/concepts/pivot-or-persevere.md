## The idea

At regular points, a team should ask a blunt question: is the evidence telling us to keep going, or to change course? A pivot changes one fundamental part of the strategy while keeping what has been learned. Persevering is also a choice, and it should be made on purpose rather than by default.

## What the source says

Eric Ries describes the pivot in *The Lean Startup* (2011) as a structured course correction designed to test a new fundamental hypothesis about the product, strategy or engine of growth. On his blog in 2009 he contrasted pivoting with abandoning the vision outright. A pivot keeps one foot in what has been learned while stepping toward a new possibility, so it builds on validated learning instead of starting over.

In the book he lists several kinds of pivot. A zoom-in pivot turns one feature into the whole product, and a zoom-out pivot does the reverse. Other kinds change the target customer segment, the customer need being addressed, the platform, the business architecture, how value is captured, the engine of growth, the channel, or the underlying technology. Each changes one element while holding others steady.

Ries argues that persevering without evidence is the more common failure. Teams that get some traction but not enough can linger for years in a kind of limbo, neither failing nor succeeding. He recommends scheduling pivot-or-persevere meetings in advance, informed by [innovation accounting](/library/innovation-accounting), so the decision happens even when nobody wants to raise it.

## Keel's perspective

The scheduling advice is the part worth stealing. Without a set date and a set of numbers, the pivot question only comes up in a crisis, when it is too late to answer well. A recurring check, monthly for a young product, with thresholds written down beforehand, keeps the decision honest.

Cheap building cuts both ways. Pivots cost less to execute when an agent can rebuild a product's surface in days, which removes one excuse for persevering. It also makes thrashing easier: switching direction every few weeks without learning anything is not pivoting. A pivot should name the hypothesis that failed and the new one being tested.

## When to use it

- Growth has flattened and experiments are no longer moving the key metric.
- Some customers love one narrow part of the product and ignore the rest.
- The team keeps finding reasons to wait one more month before deciding.

## Example

For example, a team builds a general project tool for small agencies and sees mediocre retention overall. One feature, client approval links, is used heavily and shared outside the agency. At the scheduled review they decide on a zoom-in pivot: make approvals the product, test whether agencies will pay for that alone, and stop work on the rest. The hypothesis that failed was "agencies want another all-in-one tool."

## Limitations

The pivot vocabulary can make random changes sound strategic. A pivot without a clear failed hypothesis is just a restart. Some products need longer persistence than the method suggests, for example where trust, network effects or sales cycles build slowly, and quitting early is its own failure. The typology is descriptive, not a menu. Most real pivots blend several types.
