## The idea

Traditional accounting judges a business against a forecast, which tells you nothing when the business is new and the forecast is a guess. Innovation accounting judges it against itself instead. Measure where you are now, run experiments meant to move the numbers that matter, and check whether they actually moved. If they stop moving, that is the signal to rethink the strategy.

## What the source says

Eric Ries lists innovation accounting among the core principles of the Lean Startup. He presents it as the unglamorous work of measuring progress, setting milestones and prioritizing when the usual measures do not apply. In *The Lean Startup* (2011) he lays it out in three steps.

First, use a minimum viable product to establish a baseline: real data on where the business stands for each key assumption, such as conversion, retention or willingness to pay. Second, tune the engine: run a series of product changes and experiments, each intended to move those numbers from the baseline toward the ideal the business model needs. Third, decide whether to pivot or persevere. If the experiments are moving the drivers of the business model, keep going; if they are not, the strategy itself is likely wrong.

Ries ties this to his insistence on actionable metrics, measured in cohorts, rather than cumulative totals that rise regardless of whether the product is improving.

## Keel's perspective

The method's main contribution is honesty about the baseline. Many teams never measure where they started, so every later number looks like progress. Writing down the starting point, and the number the business would need to be viable, turns a vague sense of momentum into a gap you can watch close or not.

The "tune the engine" step also deserves a warning. A team can spend months polishing conversion by a point here and there and call it progress, while the gap to a viable model stays wide. The third step exists to stop that. If tuning is not closing the gap at a meaningful rate, the answer is a different strategy, not a better button.

## When to use it

- Reporting progress to yourself, a cofounder or investors before revenue is meaningful.
- Planning a quarter of experiments on an early product.
- Preparing for a scheduled [pivot-or-persevere](/library/pivot-or-persevere) decision.

## Example

For example, a team selling a bookkeeping assistant to freelancers establishes a baseline from its first cohort: a small share connect a bank account, and fewer still are active after a month. They estimate the business needs several times that retention to work. Over two months, onboarding changes raise activation, but month-one retention in new cohorts barely moves. The engine is not tuning toward the ideal, which points to a pivot conversation rather than more onboarding work.

## Limitations

The method needs enough users for cohort numbers to mean something. With very small samples, it can produce precise-looking noise. Choosing the "ideal" target involves its own guesses about the business model. It also works best for products with measurable engagement loops. For deep-tech, regulated or long-sales-cycle products, the baseline arrives slowly, and progress may need qualitative milestones alongside the numbers.
