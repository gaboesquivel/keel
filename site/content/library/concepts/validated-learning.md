## The idea

A new product is mostly a set of unproven beliefs about who wants it and why. Validated learning counts progress as the number of those beliefs you have confirmed or killed with evidence from what real customers do. Shipping, effort and opinions do not count on their own.

## What the source says

Eric Ries makes validated learning one of the five principles of the Lean Startup. His argument is that startups exist to learn how to build a sustainable business, and that this learning can be demonstrated empirically rather than asserted after the fact. Teams routinely explain failures by saying they "learned a lot." Ries treats that as a rationalization unless the learning shows up in data from actual customers, typically as a measurable change in behavior after a deliberate experiment.

In *The Lean Startup* he contrasts this with traditional milestones such as features completed or a launch delivered on schedule. A team can hit every one of those and still be building something nobody wants. Validated learning replaces them as the unit of progress, and the rest of the method (the Build-Measure-Learn loop, minimum viable products, innovation accounting) exists to produce it efficiently.

## Keel's perspective

The useful discipline here is the word "validated." It forces a team to say in advance what result would change its mind, then to look at behavior rather than interviews, compliments or internal enthusiasm. Without that, "we learned" becomes whatever story fits the outcome.

For small teams working with agents, the risk is a new kind of waste. Output is now cheap and abundant, so a week can produce a lot of working software and very little knowledge. A useful weekly check is to write down what you now know about customers that you did not know last week, and the evidence for each item. If the list is empty, the week went into output, however impressive it looks.

## When to use it

- Reviewing a sprint, a month or a quarter of early product work.
- Deciding whether a team is making progress when revenue is still near zero.
- Framing an experiment: write the belief, the behavior that would confirm it, and the behavior that would refute it.

## Example

For example, a team believes small agencies will switch from spreadsheets to its scheduling tool. A weak statement of learning would be "agencies liked the demo." A validated version would be "of the agencies we onboarded, most stopped updating their spreadsheet within two weeks, and those that did not all had the same missing integration." The second statement can be wrong, which is what makes it worth having.

## Limitations

Not everything worth knowing is visible in short-term behavior. Brand, trust and long-cycle purchases can take longer to show up than an experiment runs. Early samples are small, so "validated" often means "suggested." Treat results as evidence that shifts confidence, not as proof. The idea can also be misused to justify endless testing in place of commitment. Learning is only progress if it changes what you do next.
