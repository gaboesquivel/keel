## The idea

Customers will always ask for more than a product should do. Rather than logging, counting and grooming every request, read each one, understand it, and then let it go. The requests that matter come back again and again without anyone tracking them; the ones that never return were probably not important.

## What the source says

In Getting Real, 37signals say they welcome feature requests, and that most of what they add starts as one. They still answer no at first, and they do not store or manage requests at all. Their reasoning comes from experience: early in Basecamp's life they kept a to-do list of requested features with a tally of how often each was asked for, intending to work down it one day. They never looked at it again, because customers kept repeating the important requests, so the team always knew what mattered next. The book closes the point by noting that frequency alone does not oblige them to build something; sometimes declining protects the product's vision.

Ryan Singer's Shape Up, written at the same company more than a decade later, makes the same argument against backlogs in general. Ideas are cheap and accumulate; really important ones resurface, and anyone who cares about an idea can track it in their own way and argue for it again. There is no central list to groom.

## Keel's perspective

The insight is about signal, not about forgetting. A tracked list converts every passing request into an obligation and gives old, context-free entries the same standing as live problems. Recurrence is a cheap, honest filter: it rewards problems that keep hurting people, and it brings each one back with fresh context rather than as a stale line item.

What to discard is the requested solution. What is worth keeping, in someone's head or notes, is the problem and the situation behind the request. Listening closely while refusing to keep a ledger is harder than it sounds, and it is the listening that makes the method work.

## When to use it

- A request backlog has grown into something nobody reads but everyone feels guilty about.
- Prioritization meetings spend more time on old tickets than on current problems.
- The product has an active, vocal customer base whose repeated asks are easy to notice.
- A small team without the capacity to triage a large queue.

## Example

For example, a two-person team running a scheduling app stops logging feature requests in their issue tracker. They still read and reply to every message. Within a few weeks, one theme is obvious without any tally: people keep asking how to block out holidays. That becomes the next piece of work. A dozen other requests, each asked once, quietly disappear, and nobody misses them.

## Limitations

Recurrence measures who talks, not who matters. Loud segments dominate, while a small but strategic group, or users who churn quietly, may never repeat themselves. Bugs and compliance issues need tracking regardless of how often they are reported. The method also depends on someone reading the incoming stream continuously; in a larger organization where requests are filtered through sales or support, the repetition may never reach the people deciding. Shape Up's answer is decentralized lists rather than none at all, which is a useful middle ground.
