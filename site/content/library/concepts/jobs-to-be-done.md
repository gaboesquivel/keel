## The idea

Customers do not buy a product because of who they are. They buy it because something in their situation calls for progress, and the product looks like the best way to make it. Study that situation and the progress sought, and you learn what the product is really competing against and why people switch.

## What the source says

Jobs to Be Done is not one method but a family, and its camps disagree about what a "job" is.

**Jobs as progress (Christensen, Moesta).** Clayton Christensen and colleagues, in a 2016 Harvard Business Review article and the book *Competing Against Luck*, argue that innovation fails because companies study customer attributes and correlations instead of causes. The cause of a purchase is a job: the progress a person is trying to make in particular circumstances. Jobs have functional, social and emotional dimensions. The best-known illustration is a fast-food chain that found people bought milkshakes for different jobs at different times of day, and that improving it for the morning commuter meant competing with other things people eat on the way to work, not with other milkshakes. Bob Moesta, who worked with Christensen, turned this into a research practice. His firm teaches switch interviews: talk to people who recently changed what they use, and reconstruct the timeline of that decision. The decision is shaped by four forces. The push of a struggling moment and the pull of a better future work toward the switch; anxiety about the new option and the habit of the old one work against it.

**Jobs as tasks with measurable outcomes (Ulwick).** Tony Ulwick's Outcome-Driven Innovation treats a job as the task or goal a customer, the job executor, is trying to accomplish. Each job is broken into dozens of desired outcomes, which are stable, solution-free measures of success. Customers rate each outcome's importance and their current satisfaction in a survey, and an opportunity algorithm points to the underserved outcomes. Ulwick's firm, Strategyn, states that he conceived the approach around 1990, introduced Christensen to it, and that Christensen then popularized it in *The Innovator's Solution* (2003). Who originated what is contested between the camps.

Practitioners have argued that the two definitions are incompatible; others use switch interviews for the why and outcome measures for the what.

## Keel's perspective

The idea worth keeping from both camps is that the unit of analysis is the situation, not the person or the product. That reframes competition: a scheduling tool competes with a shared spreadsheet and a group chat, not just other scheduling tools. It is the bridge from discovery to [positioning](/library/positioning).

For small teams, the progress camp is usually more practical. Ten switch interviews are within reach; a statistically valid outcome survey across a market rarely is. Ulwick's precision becomes useful later, when a product is established and the question is which of many needs to improve next.

## When to use it

- Working out why people actually switched to (or away from) your product.
- Finding the real competitive alternatives before positioning.
- Deciding what a product is for when its users look demographically unrelated.

## Example

For example, a team selling note-taking software interviews eight recent customers about the week they signed up. Several describe the same moment: a meeting where nobody could find what had been agreed last time. The push is embarrassment in front of a client, the anxiety is moving years of notes. The team stops marketing "better notes" and builds onboarding around importing old meeting records.

## Limitations

The word "job" is used so loosely that two teams can both "do JTBD" and mean unrelated things; say which version you mean. Job statements are easy to write and hard to support with evidence. The milkshake story is a teaching illustration retold many times, and its details vary between tellings. And the forces model explains switching better than it explains first adoption in a category that does not yet exist.
