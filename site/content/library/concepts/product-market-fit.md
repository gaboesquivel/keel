## The idea

A product has found its market when people want it badly enough that demand starts doing work the team used to do by hand. Before that point, nearly every other improvement is premature. After it, the company can survive many mistakes. Fit is a matter of degree, not a moment, and it is easier to recognize than to measure.

## What the source says

Marc Andreessen's 2007 essay ranks the three things that make a startup: team, product and market. He argues that market matters most. In a great market, customers pull a merely workable product out of the company; in a bad one, a great team with a great product still fails. He credits Andy Rachleff with crystallizing this view, names the conclusion after him (getting to fit is the only thing that matters), and defines product/market fit as "being in a good market with a product that can satisfy that market."

Andreessen describes how fit feels from the inside. Without it, customers aren't getting much value, word of mouth is quiet, usage grows slowly, sales cycles drag and deals stall. With it, customers buy as fast as you can supply, money piles up, you hire to keep up, and reporters and investors come calling. His prescription is blunt: before fit, do whatever it takes to get there, including changing people, rewriting the product or moving to a different market.

Sean Ellis later offered a way to estimate fit with a survey. In a 2009 interview he described asking users how they would feel if they could no longer use the product. If more than about 40% say very disappointed, he tends to consider fit found. He was open that the threshold came from looking at many companies, roughly 150, and that the true line might be a few points either side.

## Keel's perspective

Andreessen's signs are vivid but describe the far end of the spectrum. Most small products live in the middle: a narrow group of users who would genuinely miss it, surrounded by many who wouldn't notice. That narrow group is the most useful thing to find. Ellis's survey is valuable less for its threshold than for the follow-up question of who answered "very disappointed", because that tells you who the product is really for.

Fit is also specific to a segment. A product can fit one audience well and another not at all, and the honest description names the segment.

## When to use it

- When deciding whether to invest in growth, hiring or scale, or keep working on the product itself.
- When weighing whether to persevere or change direction.
- When early metrics look positive but nobody can say who would be upset if the product disappeared.

## Example

For example, a small team builds a scheduling tool and surveys recent active users with Ellis's question. Overall, a quarter say they would be very disappointed. Split by user type, the figure is far higher for independent therapists and low for everyone else. The team narrows its positioning and roadmap to that group rather than averaging the signal away, and treats the overall number as a reminder that fit is not yet broad.

## Limitations

The concept is fuzzy by design. Andreessen's description is qualitative, and companies routinely claim fit on thin evidence. The 40% figure is a heuristic, not a measured law, and survey results depend heavily on who is asked: surveying only enthusiastic users inflates it. Fit can also erode as markets shift, so it is not a permanent achievement. And the market-first framing can be read as fatalism about product quality, which is not Andreessen's point: the product still has to satisfy the market.
