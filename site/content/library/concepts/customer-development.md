## The idea

Most companies have a disciplined process for building the product and none for finding out who will buy it. Customer development is that second process. Founders treat their plan as a list of guesses, take those guesses to real customers, and expect to loop back several times before the business is ready to scale.

## What the source says

Steve Blank set out the method in *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* (2005), drawing on his years in Silicon Valley startups. He argues that the classic product development model, a linear march from concept to launch, works for building the product but fails as a guide for the business. It assumes the customer and the market are known, then hires sales and marketing to execute against that assumption. When the assumption is wrong, the company has burned its cash scaling a guess.

His alternative runs in parallel with product development and has four steps:

- **Customer discovery**: founders turn their vision into testable hypotheses about the problem, the customer and the product, and test them in front of customers.
- **Customer validation**: the company checks whether it can sell repeatedly, building a sales model that can be repeated and scaled. If not enough customers buy, it goes back to discovery.
- **Customer creation**: once a model is validated, the company spends to drive demand.
- **Company building**: the organization changes from one built for learning to one built for execution.

Blank stresses that the steps are circular, not linear, and that several passes are normal. He also argues that founders, not hired staff, must do the early learning, and that spending on sales and marketing should wait for evidence of a viable business.

The practice at the center of all of it Blank compresses into "get out of the building." In a 2009 post he traces it to an early job at Ardent, where his CEO sent him out to visit customers after he defended an opinion with no evidence. His lesson from it: "There are no facts inside the building so get the heck outside."

## Keel's perspective

The durable insight is structural, not motivational. Blank does not just say "talk to customers." He says the company needs a separate, named process for the business's unknowns, with its own exit criteria, so that building the product cannot stand in for proving the business. That distinction matters more now that building is cheap. An agent can produce a working product in days, which makes it easier than ever to mistake product progress for business progress. Customer development asks a different question each week: which hypothesis about the customer did we test, and outside whose building.

## When to use it

- Before hiring for sales or marketing, or before spending on growth.
- When a team has a working product and no repeatable way to sell it.
- When a plan's customer section is written from inside the office.

## Example

For example, a two-person team builds an invoicing tool for freelance translators. Discovery means calling translators and agencies to check whether late payment is a real, frequent problem and who feels it. Validation means trying to sell a paid plan to the next twenty prospects by hand. If only two pay, the team returns to discovery rather than buying ads.

## Limitations

The four steps were written for technology startups with a sales motion; their later stages say less about consumer products that grow through usage. "Get out of the building" can turn into collecting opinions, which is why [problem interviews](/library/problem-interviews) matter: the quality of the conversation decides the quality of the evidence. Blank himself later framed the process around testing a whole business model, which [the business model search](/library/business-model-search) entry covers.
