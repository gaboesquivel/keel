## The idea

A startup is not a small version of a big company. A big company executes a business model it already knows works. A startup does not yet have one, so its real work is to search for a way of creating, delivering and capturing value that can be repeated and grown. Treating that search as if it were execution is the classic early mistake.

## What the source says

Steve Blank, in a 2010 post, defines a startup as an organization formed to search for a repeatable and scalable business model; by 2012 he describes it as a temporary organization, set against a company that executes a model it already knows. He describes a business model as how a company creates, delivers and captures value. On day one, every part of it is a hypothesis: who the customers are, how to reach them, what to charge, which partners matter, how to finance it. [Customer development](/library/customer-development) is the method for testing those hypotheses with customers, agile engineering is the method for iterating the product, and Blank notes that most startups change their model at least once, often several times.

Alexander Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur supplied the common vocabulary for what is being searched for. In *Business Model Generation* (2010), co-created with several hundred practitioners, they lay out the Business Model Canvas: nine building blocks covering customer segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partnerships and cost structure. The canvas fits on one page, so a team can describe its current model, sketch alternatives side by side, and see which blocks a change would affect. Strategyzer's own guidance treats a filled-in canvas as a claim to be revised as evidence arrives.

Blank's later writing treats the canvas's blocks as the hypotheses customer development tests: each block starts as a guess, and getting out of the building is how it becomes evidence.

## Keel's perspective

The distinction between searching and executing is the useful part. Execution tools, such as detailed plans, hiring for scale, roadmaps, quarterly targets, assume the model is known. Used during search, they turn guesses into commitments and make the team slower to change its mind. The question to ask is which mode you are in, and to be honest about it.

The canvas is helpful mainly as a checklist of what you have not tested. Most teams know a great deal about their product and very little about channels, pricing and cost structure. When building gets cheap, the product block gets filled first and fastest, and the other eight can stay guesses for a long time. A canvas with a confidence note on each block makes that imbalance visible.

## When to use it

- Deciding whether a company is ready for growth spending, formal planning or scale hiring.
- Reviewing a new venture inside an established company, which is also in search mode.
- Mapping which parts of a business are still assumptions before choosing what to test next.

## Example

For example, a team building a bookkeeping assistant for food trucks fills in a canvas. The product and value proposition rest on dozens of interviews. The channel ("food truck associations will promote us") and revenue stream ("monthly subscription") rest on nothing. The next month goes to testing those two blocks, by asking two associations for a newsletter slot and offering a paid pilot, rather than adding features.

## Limitations

The canvas describes a model at a point in time; it says little about competition, timing or sequence. Filling it in can feel like progress when it is only a well-organized guess. Ash Maurya's [Lean Canvas](/library/lean-canvas) is an adaptation aimed at early startups. The search framing also fits venture-scale ambitions best: a small, profitable business may not need a model that scales, only one that repeats.
