## The idea

Most of the people a product reaches never get value from it, and the losses happen at predictable steps. Pirate metrics name five of them: people arrive (Acquisition), have a good first experience (Activation), come back (Retention), tell others (Referral), and pay (Revenue). Measuring the conversion between steps shows where the product leaks, which is usually more useful than a single total.

## What the source says

Dave McClure presented "Startup Metrics for Pirates" in 2007, including a talk at Ignite Seattle; the name comes from the acronym AARRR. His slides define each step in terms of user behavior on a web product: users arrive from some channel, have a happy first visit, return repeatedly, like it enough to refer others, and do something that makes money.

The deck is practical rather than theoretical. McClure suggests tracking a small number of conversion steps, five to ten, with fewer being better, and improving them iteratively. He pushes for measuring marketing channels deeper than the landing page, so cost per channel is judged by who activates and stays, not who clicks. He gives illustrative activation signals such as time on site, pages viewed or use of a key feature, asks for referrals only after users have had a good experience, and suggests spending most product effort on improving existing features rather than adding new ones. His example conversion rates are explicitly labeled as not real data.

## Keel's perspective

The lasting contribution is the habit of looking at a product as a sequence of conversions rather than a pile of totals. Total sign-ups can climb while activation falls, and only the ratio reveals it. The model also makes the order of work visible: pouring money into acquisition while retention leaks wastes most of it.

For small teams, Activation and Retention deserve most of the attention early. They are the steps closest to the question of whether the product is useful at all. Referral and Revenue are partly downstream of them.

## When to use it

- When setting up product analytics for the first time and deciding what to instrument.
- When growth stalls and nobody can say which step is the bottleneck.
- When judging a marketing channel, to compare channels by downstream behavior rather than traffic.

## Example

For example, a team running a small invoicing tool sees healthy traffic and disappointing revenue. Laying out the five steps, they find most visitors sign up, but few send a first invoice within a week. That makes Activation, not Acquisition or pricing, the bottleneck. They define activation as sending one invoice, then work on the path to that moment before spending more on ads.

## Limitations

The funnel was drawn for consumer web products in 2007, and its order is not universal: some products earn revenue before retention can be observed, and referral matters little for many B2B tools. The definitions of each step are yours to choose, and a loose definition of activation makes the whole model flattering rather than informative. Five numbers also invite five owners optimizing locally; the model works best with one bottleneck chosen as the current focus.
