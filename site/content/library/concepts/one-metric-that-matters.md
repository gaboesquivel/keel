## The idea

At any moment, one number matters more than the rest, because it measures the question your business most needs answered. Name it, decide in advance what value counts as good enough, and put the team's attention on moving it. When it moves, or when you learn it was the wrong question, pick the next one.

## What the source says

Alistair Croll and Benjamin Yoskovitz argue in Lean Analytics that founders drift toward whatever looks interesting, so it is better to over-focus on one metric than to watch dozens and hope something improves. A single named metric also tells employees and investors what the company is trying to learn. Croll's post describes a squeeze-toy effect: fixing one constraint exposes the next weak spot, so the metric is expected to change.

Which metric matters depends on two things: the business model (e-commerce, SaaS, media, marketplace and so on) and the stage. The book's stages run Empathy (is there a real, painful problem and a solution people want), Stickiness (do people keep using it), Virality (do they bring others), Revenue (does the economics work), and Scale (can it grow into a sustainable business). Each stage has a gate you pass before moving on, and the focus metric belongs to the stage you are actually in.

The authors also insist on a line in the sand: a target set before you look, so the metric gives a verdict rather than a number to admire. Good metrics, in their framing, are rates or ratios rather than running totals, are comparable over time, and are tied to a decision you would make differently depending on the result.

## Keel's perspective

The value is less in the number than in the conversation it forces. To choose one metric you have to admit which stage you are in, which is often earlier than founders would like. A team still finding out whether anyone returns has no business optimizing referral loops. The stage model is a guard against measuring the right thing in the wrong order.

The line in the sand matters most. Without it, almost any movement can be narrated as progress. Writing the threshold down first turns a dashboard into a test.

## When to use it

- At the start of a cycle of work, to decide what the next few weeks are trying to move.
- When a team reports many metrics and nobody can say which one would change the plan.
- When you suspect you are optimizing a later stage (growth, monetization) before an earlier one (retention) is settled.

## Example

For example, a small team ships a habit-tracking app and has steady sign-ups but no idea whether anyone stays. They decide they are in Stickiness, choose the share of new users still active in week four as their one metric, and write down a threshold before checking. Referral prompts and pricing experiments wait. If the number clears the line, the next stage's metric takes over; if it stays far below, the team revisits the product or the audience instead of the funnel.

## Limitations

One metric can be gamed, and a team staring at one number can damage what it doesn't watch; keep a few guardrail measures alongside it. The stages are a simplification; real companies straddle them, and some revisit an earlier stage after a change in market. Thresholds borrowed from other companies are starting points, not facts about your market. And in a business with several distinct products, "one" usually means one per bet, not one for the whole company.
