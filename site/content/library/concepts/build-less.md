## The idea

The usual way to compete is to match a rival's features and then add a few. That race has no finish line and makes every product in it heavier and more alike. The alternative is to do less on purpose: solve the simpler, common problems very well, leave the hard edge cases to others, and make the smaller scope part of what the product stands for.

## What the source says

Getting Real opens its practical advice with this chapter. 37signals describe the habit of one-upping competitors, adding features and spending to stay ahead, as an expensive, defensive posture that keeps a company reacting rather than leading. Their answer is to do less than competitors in order to beat them: take on the simple problems and leave the difficult, messy ones to everyone else. They list what less means in practice, covering fewer features, fewer options and preferences, less organizational structure, fewer meetings and abstractions, and fewer promises, and they say the theme runs through the whole book.

Jason Fried and David Heinemeier Hansson return to the point in REWORK, their 2010 book on running a business, in a short essay titled "Underdo your competition" within the section on competitors. As it is widely summarized, the essay argues the same case for a broader business audience: pick a smaller set of problems, do them better, and treat the narrow scope as something to promote rather than apologize for. This entry relies on the Getting Real chapter for the detail of the argument.

## Keel's perspective

Building less is a strategic choice, not only a cost-saving one. A product that does fewer things is easier to explain, easier to learn, and easier to position against a crowded field: the gap in features becomes the difference customers notice. It also means each remaining feature can be good, which is where [Half a Product, Not a Half-Assed Product](/library/half-not-half-assed) and Build Less meet.

It matters more as building gets cheaper. When an agent can add a feature in an hour, feature parity is reachable, so everyone reaches it, and products converge. Accumulation also produces [drift](/library/product-drift): a codebase and product that slowly stop matching what anyone intended. Choosing what not to build is one of the few product decisions that still requires judgment rather than effort.

## When to use it

- Entering a market with established, feature-heavy incumbents.
- A roadmap driven by competitor comparison tables.
- Defining what a product will deliberately not do, as part of its positioning.
- Pruning an existing product whose surface has outgrown its purpose.

## Example

For example, a team entering the project-management market notices that competitors offer Gantt charts, resource planning, time tracking and custom workflows. Rather than matching them, the team targets small agencies that mainly need to share status with clients. They build only shared task lists and client-visible updates, and their marketing says plainly that the product does not do Gantt charts.

## Limitations

Underdoing works when there is a meaningful group whose needs the simpler product fully meets. In markets bought through checklists, such as enterprise procurement, missing features disqualify a product before anyone tries it. Doing less is also easy to use as cover for not doing the hard thing that would make the product valuable. The strategy needs a clear view of which problems to leave to others; without it, less is just less.
