## The idea

Discovery is usually treated as a phase: research up front, then build. Continuous discovery treats it as a habit. The team that builds the product talks with customers every week, in small pieces of research aimed at a specific outcome, so its decisions rest on recent contact rather than an old report.

## What the source says

Teresa Torres, in *Continuous Discovery Habits* (2021), defines continuous discovery as weekly touchpoints with customers, carried out by the team building the product, through small research activities in pursuit of a desired outcome. Each part of that definition does work.

**Weekly** is the minimum cadence. Customer needs and context change, and a team that learns in bursts makes most of its decisions on stale information.

**By the team building the product** rules out the common arrangement where a separate research function interviews customers and hands over findings. Torres argues that secondhand insight loses the detail that shapes good decisions, and that the people choosing what to build need direct contact.

**Small research activities** means interviews, quick assumption tests and similar lightweight steps, not large studies.

**In pursuit of a desired outcome** ties the research to a measurable target, so discovery is scoped rather than open-ended.

The unit doing this work is the product trio: a product manager, a designer and a software engineer who share responsibility for an outcome. They interview together, map the customer's needs together, choose what to pursue and test solutions together. Torres presents this as a replacement for handoffs, where misunderstandings between roles produce rework. The book builds a set of habits around this, including automated interview recruiting, story-based interviews and the [opportunity solution tree](/library/opportunity-solution-tree).

## Keel's perspective

The strongest part of the idea is the cadence. A team that talks to one customer every week will notice a wrong assumption within a month; a team that does a research project each quarter may build on it for a season. For small teams the trio often collapses into one or two people, and the habit matters even more, because there is no one else whose job it is to stay close to customers.

When agents do much of the building, the time a builder used to spend writing code can move toward customer contact. That is the right trade. The bottleneck shifts from producing software to knowing which software is worth producing, and continuous discovery is the practice that feeds that judgment.

## When to use it

- A team ships steadily but cannot say what it learned from customers this month.
- Research exists but sits in decks the builders have not read.
- A product is past its first launch and decisions are getting more incremental.

## Example

For example, a three-person team building a booking tool for physiotherapy clinics sets up an in-app prompt that schedules one 30-minute call a week with a clinic owner. Whoever is building that week joins. After each call they add what they heard to a shared map of needs, and a recurring theme about no-show reminders becomes the next opportunity they test.

## Limitations

Weekly interviewing has a real cost, and in some markets (enterprise buyers, regulated users) recruiting at that pace is hard. Torres's framework assumes a team with an outcome and some autonomy over how to reach it; in organizations that hand teams fixed roadmaps, the habit has little to act on. Methods that concentrate discovery in fewer people, such as [shaping](/library/shaping) in Shape Up, make a different trade: they protect builders' uninterrupted time at the cost of builders' direct exposure to customers.
