## The idea

Choose one measure that captures the value customers actually get from the product, the kind of number that, if it keeps rising, makes long-term business success likely. Keep it steady so every team pulls in the same direction. Because nobody can move it directly, break it into a handful of input metrics that teams can influence.

## What the source says

The idea is most often credited to Sean Ellis, the marketer widely associated with the term growth hacking. In a 2018 interview with Intercom he described the North Star as a lasting, company-wide measure of success: it quantifies the moment customers experience the product's value and is tracked over time. His examples were nights booked for Airbnb, which reflects value for both guests and hosts, rather than app installs. He placed it after product-market fit, and kept it distinct from the one metric that matters right now, which he treated as the best near-term lever for moving the North Star. Where the term first appeared is not well documented, so it is safest to say Ellis popularized it rather than invented it.

Amplitude's North Star Playbook, co-authored by John Cutler, turned the idea into a framework. Amplitude's materials define the North Star as the single metric that best captures the value customers derive from the product, and ask that it express product strategy and act as a leading indicator, sitting upstream of revenue rather than reporting it. Around it sit three to five inputs: complementary factors the team believes drive the North Star and can affect through the product, often found by looking at breadth, depth, frequency and efficiency of use.

## Keel's perspective

The useful part is the split between the North Star and its inputs. The North Star says what winning means for customers; the inputs are where work actually happens. A team that can name the input it is trying to move, and the story of how that input feeds the North Star, has a testable theory of its product. A team that only names the North Star has a slogan.

The danger is picking something that is easy to count instead of something that reflects value. Revenue, sign-ups and time spent are tempting and usually wrong as a North Star.

## When to use it

- After there is real evidence of product-market fit and several teams or workstreams need a shared definition of success.
- When roadmap debates keep stalling because people optimize different numbers.
- When you need to explain to a new contributor, human or agent, what the product is ultimately for.

## Example

For example, a team building a shared-notes tool for small agencies might choose "notes edited by two or more people per week" as its North Star, because collaborative editing is the value the product exists to deliver. Inputs could be the share of workspaces with a second active member (breadth), edits per active note (depth), and weekly return rate (frequency). One team works on invitations, another on editing, and each can show how its input should move the shared number.

## Limitations

Before product-market fit, a stable North Star is premature; you don't yet know what value customers get, and a stage-specific focus metric fits better. The causal link between inputs and the North Star is a hypothesis, and it can be wrong for a long time without anyone noticing. Consumer examples dominate the literature; in enterprise or low-frequency products, value is harder to express as one count. And a single metric, however well chosen, can still be gamed, so guardrails remain necessary.
