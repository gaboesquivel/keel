## The idea

A new product does not take off by itself. In the beginning the founders have to supply the push: finding users one at a time, looking after them far better than any large company would, and doing by hand the work the product will eventually automate. That effort is not a detour from building the company; it is how the company learns what to build.

## What the source says

Paul Graham's July 2013 essay collects advice Y Combinator gives founders. The most common unscalable thing is recruiting users manually. Founders avoid it because it feels beneath them or because the early numbers look too small to matter, but with steady weekly growth small beginnings compound. He points to Stripe's founders setting up new users on the spot rather than sending them a link.

Graham argues that early startups are fragile and should not be judged by the standards of mature companies, and that founders tend to underestimate their own. He urges them to go to great lengths to delight early users, noting that small companies can offer attention large ones cannot, and that the user's experience, not only the product, is what should be exceptional at first. Close contact with users is also where the feedback comes from.

To get growth started he recommends a deliberately narrow market that can reach critical mass, comparing it to keeping a fire contained until it burns hot before adding more fuel; Facebook beginning at Harvard is his example. Other tactics include acting almost as a consultant for a first B2B customer, assembling early hardware by hand, and running parts of the service manually behind the scenes until it is clear what to automate. He is skeptical of big launches and partnerships as a source of growth. The essay ends by suggesting the best unscalable habits, such as aggressive recruiting and caring about users, should outlast the early days.

## Keel's perspective

This is the entry for early distribution. The hard part of a new product is rarely the code; it is getting the first few dozen people to try it, and learning from them fast enough to make it good. Manual recruiting and manual service are research methods as much as growth tactics.

The idea gains weight when building is cheap. With agents, a team can produce a polished product before having a single committed user, and it is tempting to automate onboarding, support and outreach on day one. Automating too early removes exactly the contact that tells you what is wrong. Keep the first users close and the process hand-run until the pattern is obvious, then automate the part you now understand.

## When to use it

- Launching a new product or a new segment with no existing audience.
- When sign-ups trickle in and you are tempted to wait for a launch or partnership to fix it.
- Before automating onboarding, support or fulfillment, to learn what the automated version should actually do.

## Example

For example, a developer builds a bookkeeping tool for freelance photographers. Instead of announcing it widely, she contacts photographers in one city, sets up each account on a call, imports their first month of receipts by hand, and checks in a week later. Ten users take a month. The calls reveal that the real pain is sorting card transactions, not invoicing, which reshapes the roadmap. Only once setup looks the same for every user does she build a self-serve flow.

## Limitations

The advice is about the start, not a permanent operating model; a company that never stops doing things by hand cannot grow past its founders' hours. Some markets, such as products sold through large procurement cycles, offer few chances to recruit one user at a time. Delighting a handful of users can also mislead if they are unrepresentative friends or fellow founders. And manual service can mask a product that does not work, so the unscalable effort should be paired with honest attention to what users do once the founders step back.
