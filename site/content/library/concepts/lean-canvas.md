## The idea

A business plan for a new product is mostly untested guesses, so it should be short enough to rewrite in an afternoon. The Lean Canvas fits the whole model on one page, starting from the problem and the customer rather than from the product. Seeing every box together makes it obvious which guesses everything else depends on.

## What the source says

Ash Maurya created the Lean Canvas around 2010 and built *Running Lean* around it. The book grew from his blog, was self-published in 2011, and was later published by O'Reilly, with a third edition in 2022. LEANSTACK, his company, describes the canvas as an adaptation of Alexander Osterwalder's Business Model Canvas for startups working under extreme uncertainty.

The canvas keeps Osterwalder's customer segments, value proposition, channels, cost structure and revenue streams. It replaces four boxes aimed at established operations (key partners, key activities, key resources, customer relationships) with Problem, Solution, Key Metrics and Unfair Advantage. Maurya's reasoning is that an early product's biggest risk is usually building something for a problem customers do not have, so the problem deserves its own box. An unfair advantage, something hard to copy or buy, is what separates a durable business from a feature. The version LEANSTACK publishes today also prompts for existing alternatives, early adopters and a one-line high-level concept.

In *Running Lean*, the canvas is the starting point for a process: write it quickly, identify the riskiest parts, and test them with customers, updating the canvas as evidence comes in.

## Keel's perspective

The canvas is most useful as a diagnostic, not a deliverable. Its worth comes from the empty or vague boxes. If "Unfair Advantage" says "great UX" and "Existing Alternatives" is blank, the canvas has told you where to look.

Write several canvases, not one. Different customer segments often imply different problems, channels and pricing. Comparing two or three one-page versions is a cheap way to choose which to test first, and it keeps the team from treating its first guess as the plan.

## When to use it

- Before building, to put an idea's assumptions on one page.
- When comparing customer segments or variants of an idea.
- As a reference point to revisit at each pivot-or-persevere review.

## Example

For example, a developer has an idea for a tool that flags risky database migrations before deploy. A first canvas aimed at large enterprises shows long sales cycles in Channels and a weak Unfair Advantage. A second, aimed at small teams on one popular framework, shows a clear early adopter, an existing alternative (manual review), and a channel through the framework's community. The second is cheaper to test, so it goes first.

## Limitations

A filled-in canvas can look like knowledge when it is all guesses. The format invites confident short phrases. It compresses away operational realities, partners and key activities among them, that matter for hardware, marketplaces or regulated products, where Osterwalder's original canvas may fit better. It also says little about how to test each box. For that, pair it with [riskiest assumption first](/library/riskiest-assumption-first) and direct customer conversations.
