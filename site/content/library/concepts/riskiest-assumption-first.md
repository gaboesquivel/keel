## The idea

Every new product rests on a handful of beliefs that have to be true for it to work. They are not equally dangerous. Rank them by how uncertain they are and how much breaks if they are wrong, then spend your next effort testing the top one, as a hypothesis that could clearly fail.

## What the source says

Eric Ries calls these beliefs leap-of-faith assumptions in *The Lean Startup* (2011) and singles out two as the most important: the value hypothesis (customers actually get value from the product once they use it) and the growth hypothesis (new customers will find it, by whatever engine of growth). He argues that early experiments should target these leaps directly, because the rest of the plan is built on top of them.

Ash Maurya, in *Running Lean* and his later writing, puts the prioritization step at the center. In a 2023 piece on identifying riskiest assumptions he argues that teams usually pick what is easy to test, not what is risky. He also says gut feel, group voting and 2x2 risk matrices are too subjective to rank risk well. He proposes a more systematic approach that borrows from the Theory of Constraints, locating the weakest step in the business model and tracing the assumptions behind it.

Rik Higham's 2016 post "The MVP is dead. Long live the RAT." argues that the MVP label pulls teams toward building a product when what they need is the smallest test of their riskiest assumption, which he calls a Riskiest Assumption Test.

## Keel's perspective

The value is in the ordering. Most teams can list their assumptions; few test them in order of danger, because the riskiest ones are often uncomfortable ("will anyone pay?") while the easy ones feel productive ("can we build the integration?"). When agents make building cheap, feasibility risk shrinks further and the remaining risk concentrates on desirability and viability. Testing feasibility first then becomes more of a hiding place, not less.

A good test is written so it can fail. "Users will find it useful" cannot fail. "At least a third of trial users will invite a teammate in their first week" can. Decide the threshold before you see the data.

## When to use it

- At the start of any product or major feature, before writing a plan.
- When a backlog of experiments exists and you need to pick one.
- When a team is busy and confident but cannot name what would prove them wrong.

## Example

For example, a team plans a marketplace connecting small clinics with locum nurses. Their assumptions include that clinics have urgent unfilled shifts, that nurses will accept the fee, that payments can be automated, and that clinics will trust unvetted profiles. Payments are the hardest to build but the least uncertain. Clinic trust is the most uncertain and fatal if wrong, so the first test is manually matching a few shifts and watching whether clinics accept the nurses offered.

## Limitations

Ranking risk is itself a judgment, and teams tend to underrate the risks they do not understand. Some assumptions cannot be tested cheaply or quickly, such as regulatory approval or long-term retention, and need other forms of diligence. Testing one assumption at a time can also miss interactions: a product can pass each test separately and still fail as a whole.
