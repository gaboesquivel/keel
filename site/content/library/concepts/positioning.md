## The idea

Customers judge a product by comparing it with what they would otherwise do. That alternative is often not a rival product but a spreadsheet, a manual routine or doing nothing. Good positioning starts there: name the real alternative, show what you do that it cannot, and choose the market frame that makes that difference obvious.

## What the source says

April Dunford, in *Obviously Awesome* (2019), treats positioning as the context a company sets for its product so that the right customers immediately see why it matters. She breaks it into components that depend on one another: competitive alternatives, the unique attributes the product has that those alternatives lack, the value those attributes deliver, the customers who care most about that value, and the market category the product is presented in.

Her starting point is competitive alternatives. In a 2020 post she frames the question directly: "What would a customer do if your offering didn't exist?" The first alternative to beat is usually the status quo: spreadsheets, documents, manual processes or the limited features of a system the customer already owns. These are cheap, familiar and good enough, and a large share of lost deals, she notes, go to no decision rather than to a competitor. Beyond the status quo, the competitors that matter are the ones prospects actually shortlist. Companies that never come up in deals are what she calls phantom competitors, and positioning against them dilutes the message.

From the alternatives, Dunford works forward. Differentiation is only meaningful relative to what the customer would otherwise use, so a feature is not a selling point unless the alternative lacks it. Value is what that difference does for the customer. The target segment is the group that cares most about that value. The market category comes late in the process and is chosen deliberately, as a frame of reference that tells buyers what to compare the product with and which strengths to expect.

## Keel's perspective

This is the right way for small teams to think about competition and differentiation. A founder's list of competitors is usually the products they admire or fear. The customer's list is shorter and plainer, and it often starts with "keep doing what we do now." Interviewing for the real alternative, ideally with [jobs-to-be-done](/library/jobs-to-be-done) switch interviews, gives positioning something solid to stand on.

It also guards against a failure that cheap building makes worse. When features are easy to add, products drift toward parity with every competitor in view. Positioning asks which few attributes produce value the alternative cannot, and that answer is also a guide to what not to build.

## When to use it

- Sales conversations stall because prospects do not see why to change.
- A product is described with the same words as everyone else in its space.
- Choosing which features to emphasize, or which to build next.
- Before writing a homepage, pitch or launch announcement.

## Example

For example, a team building a tool for tracking equipment loans in university labs first positions it as "asset management software" and gets compared with large enterprise suites. Interviews show the real alternative is a shared spreadsheet that goes out of date. They reposition around what the spreadsheet cannot do, such as knowing who has the item right now and reminding them to return it, and describe it as a lab sign-out system. Prospects stop asking for enterprise features they never needed.

## Limitations

Dunford's method is built mainly from business-to-business software, where buyers compare options deliberately; consumer products often win on habit, brand or distribution instead. Positioning clarifies what you already have; it cannot rescue a product with no real advantage over the alternative. And the right category can change as a market matures, so a positioning that was correct at launch needs revisiting.
