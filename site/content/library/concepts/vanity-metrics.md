## The idea

A metric is worth tracking only if it can change a decision. Cumulative totals like all-time signups or page views tend to go up no matter what you do, so they flatter everyone and inform no one. Actionable metrics connect a specific change to a specific effect, usually by looking at comparable groups of customers.

## What the source says

Eric Ries set out the distinction in a 2009 guest post on Tim Ferriss's blog. Vanity metrics such as raw hits feel good but give no guidance. Actionable metrics reveal cause and effect. His suggested techniques were split tests, which confirm or refute a specific hypothesis; per-customer and per-segment metrics in place of aggregates; funnel and cohort analysis, which follow groups who joined in the same period; and treating acquisition channels such as search keywords as segments. He also urged measuring as few things as possible and keeping metrics traceable to real people, so a number can prompt a follow-up conversation.

In a December 2009 post on his own blog, Ries added an organizational argument. Because a vanity metric cannot attribute cause, every team claims credit when it rises and blames others when it falls, and the company splinters into competing stories. In *The Lean Startup* (2011) he gathers the qualities of good reports under three A's: actionable (clear cause and effect), accessible (simple enough that people actually use them), and auditable (the data can be checked back to its source and trusted).

## Keel's perspective

The test is simple: before adding a number to a dashboard, write the decision it would change if it moved. If you cannot, it is decoration. Most vanity metrics are not false, they just answer a question nobody asked.

This applies to building too. Lines of code, tickets closed and features shipped are vanity metrics for product progress, and they are especially seductive when an agent can produce large volumes of all three. Progress is better measured by what customers do differently, as in [validated learning](/library/validated-learning).

## When to use it

- Designing a dashboard or an investor update.
- Reviewing whether a launch or change actually worked.
- When a metric keeps rising but the business does not feel healthier.

## Example

For example, a newsletter tool reports 20,000 total signups, up every month. Split into weekly cohorts, the picture changes: recent cohorts send their first issue less often than older ones did, after an onboarding redesign. The total hid a regression. The cohort view points to a cause and a decision: revisit the redesign.

## Limitations

Vanity metrics are not always useless. Top-of-funnel totals can be leading indicators and matter for awareness or fundraising. The problem is using them to judge whether the product is working. Actionable metrics need volume. With a few dozen users, cohorts and split tests are noisy, and talking to customers often teaches more. A metric can also be actionable and still be the wrong target, which is why it pairs well with choosing [one metric that matters](/library/one-metric-that-matters).
